CHAPTER 0
ABSTRACT
================================================================================

During disasters, social media is often the first source of eyewitness reports and requests for help, but in India these posts span many languages and are too numerous and unverified to act on directly. This project presents a multilingual system that turns posts in English, Hindi, Kannada, Tamil and Telugu into verified, prioritised situations for government departments. Posts are cleaned and language-tagged by a two-stage detector (99.24% accuracy), and fine-tuned XLM-RoBERTa models filter disaster-related posts (89.02% accuracy), classify sentiment (82.75%) and detect department needs (micro-F1 0.73). Related posts are grouped using IndicSBERT embeddings, and each situation receives explainable priority and fake-news scores, a multilingual summary and privacy-masked evidence. Human reviewers confirm situations before tickets are routed to every relevant department, and urgent situations are escalated automatically if not reviewed in time. The results show that multilingual crisis posts can be turned into timely, human-validated alerts.

CHAPTER 3
LITERATURE REVIEW
(Target length: 3-4 pages)
================================================================================

This chapter reviews published work related to the main tasks of the system. The studies are grouped by task: multilingual and code-mixed text analysis, disaster-related post detection, the classification of crisis needs, and the grouping and prioritisation of reports. For each study, the approach is described first, followed by its key findings and limitations. The chapter closes with the research gap that motivates this project.


3.1 SURVEY OF EXISTING WORK

3.1.1 Multilingual and Code-Mixed Text Analysis

Bandarupalli [1] compared traditional, recurrent and transformer models for sentiment analysis of multilingual social media posts. TF-IDF with Logistic Regression, LSTMs with GloVe and FastText embeddings, and a fine-tuned mBERT were evaluated on a synthetic dataset of 10,000 posts in English, Hindi and Spanish.
  •	Key findings: mBERT performed best and handled code-switching, slang and emojis effectively.
  •	Limitations: the data is synthetic and does not cover real dialect variation.

Rakshit et al. [2] applied RNN, Bi-RNN and LSTM models with Word2Vec embeddings to classify the sentiment of large Twitter datasets.
  •	Key findings: the LSTM performed best, with about 83.5% accuracy, and keeping stopwords improved the interpretation of sentiment.
  •	Limitations: English only, no fine-grained sentiment classes, and no modern contextual models such as BERT.

Kumar et al. [3] proposed two multitask models that extend XLM-R with FNet and FastFormer layers to classify the sentiment and emotion of Hindi–English code-mixed tweets from the SemEval-2020 SentiMix dataset.
  •	Key findings: XLMR-FNet performed best, with weighted F1 scores of 71.37 for sentiment and 68.01 for emotion.
  •	Limitations: evaluated on Hindi–English data only; the added layers increase computational cost; no crisis-specific need detection or routing.

Chen et al. [4] improved zero-shot cross-lingual transfer by combining the lower and upper layers of mBERT through attention-based fusion, so that a model trained in one language works better in others.
  •	Key findings: performance improved by 1–3%. The lower layers helped transfer to new languages, and the upper layers supported syntactic understanding.
  •	Limitations: training relies on English only, crisis content is not considered, and the fusion adds computational load.

3.1.2 Disaster-Related Post Detection

Algiriyage et al. [5] compared a wide range of machine-learning and deep-learning methods for identifying disaster-related tweets across many crisis events.
  •	Key findings: a Bi-LSTM with Word2Vec achieved the highest F1 score of about 0.92. Deep-learning models generally outperformed traditional ones, and training on several events improved generalisation.
  •	Limitations: English only, binary labels, offline evaluation, no need detection, and weaker performance when moving to a different disaster type.

Chinta et al. [6] combined deep-learning models with a streaming pipeline to monitor social media and detect crisis indicators in real time.
  •	Key findings: accuracy and F1 of about 0.88, with crisis signals identified within minutes by combining sentiment, engagement and trend patterns.
  •	Limitations: no multilingual support, no classification of specific needs, no routing, and sensitivity to misinformation and classification errors.

3.1.3 Classification of Crisis Needs

Ray Chowdhury et al. [7] combined existing crisis datasets, including CrisisLexT26 and CrisisNLP, into a multilingual multi-label dataset of over 130,000 tweets. Multilingual BERT was fine-tuned with Manifold Mixup and a masked loss for partially labelled tweets.
  •	Key findings: Manifold Mixup improved the model on all four test sets, with F1 scores of 79.84 to 87.44, and generalised to disasters not seen in training. Zero-shot F1 on languages absent from training was 85.26 for Spanish, 81.33 for French and 75.44 for Italian.
  •	Limitations: mainly English and European languages with no Indian languages; only four broad information classes; classification only, with no sentiment analysis, prioritisation or routing.

Das et al. [8] built a multi-label dataset of 2,400 Bengali tweets from Cyclone Amphan labelled with humanitarian categories. Fine-tuned multilingual transformers (XLM-R, MuRIL, mBERT and IndicBERT) were compared with general-purpose LLMs (GPT-3.5-Turbo and GPT-4-Turbo).
  •	Key findings: this was the first study to classify Bengali disaster tweets into humanitarian categories, and both the fine-tuned models and the LLMs classified them reasonably well.
  •	Limitations: one language, one disaster and a small dataset; classification only, with no grouping, prioritisation or departmental routing.

3.1.4 Grouping and Prioritisation of Reports

Ray et al. [13] detected events in a Twitter stream with an incremental clustering algorithm based on sentence embeddings. Each new tweet joins the most similar existing cluster if it is similar enough, and otherwise starts a new one.
  •	Key findings: on 68,841 English tweets covering 503 events, the method reached an ARI of 0.63 and an AMI of 0.86, improving on earlier baselines at low computational cost.
  •	Limitations: English tweets only; events are detected but not scored, verified or routed.

Adesokan and Madria [14] proposed CURD, a system that filters irrelevant posts and determines the urgency of relevant ones. Its classifier combines transformer models with a convolution layer and custom attention, trained on tweets labelled through a multi-level annotation process.
  •	Key findings: on 47,621 disaster tweets, urgency was classified with an F1 score of 0.88 and an AUC of 0.94.
  •	Limitations: urgency is a binary label assigned to individual tweets; the method needs urgency-labelled data; related reports are not grouped.

Ech-Chammakhy et al. [15] presented EventHunter, which clusters hacker-forum discussions into security events and ranks the events with a weighted score.
  •	Key findings: dynamic clustering grouped related discussions into events, and the weighted score ordered the events by importance in an interpretable way.
  •	Limitations: the domain is cybersecurity rather than crises; the ranking was assessed only qualitatively; there is no misinformation check or human review.


3.2 RESEARCH GAP

The surveyed studies show that transformer models handle multilingual and code-mixed text well, with XLM-R and mBERT outperforming earlier LSTM and TF-IDF approaches [1], [3]. However, each study addresses only one stage of crisis monitoring, and together they leave the following gaps:
  •	Language coverage: the sentiment and disaster-detection studies are English-only or limited to Hindi–English [2], [3], [5], [6], and multilingual need classification has been shown mainly for European languages [7].
  •	Need detection: the sentiment studies analyse general emotion and do not identify what help people need [1], [2], [3].
  •	Indian-language crisis data: the closest study classifies Bengali posts from a single cyclone and stops at classification [8].
  •	Grouping and ranking: clustering and urgency methods work on English posts or on other domains, and none checks reports for misinformation or passes them to a human reviewer [13], [14], [15].

No surveyed system combines Indian-language analysis, need detection, grouping of related reports into situations, priority and misinformation scoring, and verified routing to government departments. This project addresses that gap.

4.1 Introduction
The reason for the existence of this specification document is that it needs to describe, in an unambiguous way, the requirements for the system that need to be fulfilled as well as the attributes that it will have. This will serve as the basis for evaluating the system.
4.1.1 Project scope
The system takes in an endless stream of posts on social media, which are processed almost immediately and are turned into structured "situations" that have been prioritised in a way such that the government departments can take action on them. The scope of the project includes:
•	Ingesting posts from either a social media feed or a simulation feed into streaming queue.
•	Preprocessing: text cleaning, duplicate and repost detection, and language identification for English, Hindi, Kannada, Tamil and Telugu texts.
•	Analysis of each post: disaster relevance classification, sentiment analysis, and multi-label departmental need detection, combined into severity and actionability estimates.
•	Semantic clustering of related posts into clusters using multilingual sentence embeddings.
•	Cluster-level scoring of priority, credibility and misinformation (fake-news) risk.
•	Generation of a concise situation summary and extraction of supporting evidence.
•	A human-in-the-loop review console in which a reviewer approves each situation, marks it as fake or skips it.
•	Routing of approved situations to the responsible department as tickets with a tracked lifecycle, together with time-bound auto-escalation for critical cases.
•	An analytics dashboard presenting trends, heatmaps, response-time metrics and daily situation reports. 
4.2 Product perspective
The system is a new stand-alone product. The system is not a part of some larger system; rather, it will be positioned between social media channels and command centres within the organization. This product will utilize three layers of implementation: a presentation layer written using React; an application layer implemented as a modular monolith based on FastAPI (one router for each pipeline stage); and a persistence layer using MongoDB. Machine learning models will be loaded into memory by the application layer.

Lastly, no private data can be processed and personal identifiers cannot be stored in plain text format.

4.3 Functional Requirements
FR-01  Post Ingestion and Validation: The system must be able to receive social media posts using the ingestion endpoint, reject posts based on the rules, and add a unique identifier for each.
FR-02  Bounded Queueing and Continuous Processing: Posts that have been accepted into the system will be queued using a FIFO queue whose size is configurable. When the queue is full, the system will drop the oldest post from the queue. 
FR-03  Text Preprocessing and Duplicate Detection: The system will clean each post through either normalization or removal of links, mentions, hashtags, emojis and will check for duplicates and reposts through a text fingerprint to ensure the same text does not go through analysis again.
FR-04  Multilingual Language Identification: The system should determine the language of every post in English, Hindi, Kannada, Tamil, or Telugu languages.
FR-05  Disaster Relevance Filtering: The system shall classify each post as disaster-related or not, and only disaster-related posts shall proceed to further.
FR-06  Sentiment and Need Detection: The system should be able to identify the sentiment and negativity of every post, recognize the department expressed in every post, assign them a multi-label classification with a confidence level, and then integrate those into a single record.
FR-07  Event Clustering: The system should produce embeddings for each analyzed post and place into the most similar pre-existing cluster, if the criteria are fulfilled, otherwise a new cluster will be formed.
FR-08  Situation Creation: The system shall promote a cluster to a situation, with status pending, once it reaches the significance threshold.
FR-09  Priority and Fake Scoring: The system shall compute a priority score and a fake score between 0 and 1 for each situation. The priority score shall be mapped to one of four bands: Critical, High, Medium or Low. A situation whose fake score reaches the flag threshold of 0.60 shall be flagged as likely misinformation.
FR-10  Summarisation and Evidence Extraction: The system must provide a brief, comprehensible summary of the situation, choose the most relevant and important postings to serve as evidence.
FR-11  Human Review: system shall present pending situations to a reviewer ordered by priority, showing the summary and other information, and shall allow the reviewer to approve the situation, mark it as fake or skip it. Approved situations are routed as verified tickets; situations marked as fake are routed with unverified tickets flagged as fake, so the department knows the reviewer doubted them.
FR-12  Departmental Routing and Ticket Tracking: Upon its approval, the process will then channel the issue to the appropriate department. Officers will have the capability of transitioning from routed to acknowledged to resolved status.
FR-13  Analytics and Reporting: The system will allow a dashboard that will display number of situations per status and per department, hourly trend analysis, heat maps based on hours and the daily situation report will be available for the date selected.
4.4 Non-Functional Requirements
NFR-01  Real-Time Processing: There will be no wait time between post validation and ingestion to avoid any delays due to analysis. The background worker will poll the queue every 0.5 seconds.
NFR-02  Dashboard Response Time: Dashboard and Analytics API requests should respond within 2 seconds for the amount of data that would be processed in a normal day.
NFR-03  Privacy of Personal Data: Only those posts that are publicly visible will be processed. No author information shall ever be stored in plaintext format and must be converted into a salted SHA-256 hash. Phone numbers and other such personal details in the evidence text must be sanitized.
NFR-04  Scalability: The queue interface, worker, and storage must be designed in such a way that the in-memory queue is replaceable by a distributed message broker systems.
NFR-05  Durability: Persist all cases, review decisions, and tickets in the database such that the state of the system is maintained after a restart of the application.

CHAPTER 5

SYSTEM DESIGN
The architecture of the system has been developed using an event-driven, real-time approach based on the pipeline architecture that is used for transforming continuous input streams of data into useful information. In this model, the system is based on event-oriented design, whereby social media posts received by the system act as events that start off the processing chain. This provides a stream of continuous data, with analysis done in real time and without having to wait for batch processing. Moreover, since the system works in real time, it will ensure that all crises that arise are identified immediately, which can be important when taking action.
Figure 5.1 – HLD Diagram
  
5.1 High Level Design
5.1.2 Data Ingestion Layer
This layer obtains social media posts from the external source. In the existing prototype design, the simulator acts as an input that plays the actual crisis posts obtained from a CSV file. The post is validated, and if the post fails validation according to the rule, it is rejected/dropped. The accepted posts are put into a bounded first-in, first-out queue with a capacity of 500 posts by default. In case of queue overflow during surges, the oldest post is dropped, since the latest post is most relevant in a crisis situation.
5.1.3 Preprocessing and Analysis Layer
A background worker keeps running all the time. It takes posts from the queue. Gets them ready for analysis. Every post goes through a cleaning step first. Broken Unicode characters are fixed. Emojis are changed into their text meaning. URLs and user mentions are removed. Unnecessary symbols are stripped away. Then the system looks for reposts. It uses a text fingerprint to find them and removes those duplicates. After that the language of the remaining posts is checked. The system detects if the text is, in English, Hindi, Kannada, Tamil or Telugu. For scripts it uses script detection. For Latin-script text it uses a tuned XLM-RoBERTa model. Posts that mix writing or use code-mixed text are flagged. Finally, a disaster classifier looks at each post. Removes those that are not related to a crisis. For every post that gets dropped the reason is recorded. This helps with monitoring the process.
5.1.4 Machine Learning Pipeline, Result and Human review Interface
Posts that pass the disaster filter are processed by the machine learning pipeline, which consists of the following stages:
  
•	Sentiment analysis: A fine-tuned XLM-RoBERTa model classifies each post as negative or positive and produces a negativity score
•	Need detection: A multi-label XLM-RoBERTa model estimates the need for Healthcare, Public Municipal, Law & Order and Tourism services. A single post can express more than one need
•	Event clustering: IndicSBERT embeddings are used to group posts describing the same event, based on semantic similarity, time, department overlap and place names.
•	Cluster scoring: Once a cluster contains at least three posts, it becomes a situation. Its credibility, misinformation risk and priority are then calculated. Misinformation risk combines inverse credibility, content risk and amplification risk, and a situation scoring 0.60 or above is flagged as likely fake.
•	Summarisation: A template-based method generates a summary in five languages using only facts computed from the cluster, so the summary cannot state unsupported details.
•	Evidence extraction: Up to five independent posts are selected based on their similarity to the cluster and their strength as evidence. Personal details are masked before storage
5.1.5 Dash-board and UI interface layer
The dashboard allows decision makers to have all the information on the situations and actions taken on them at hand. Situation numbers, department workload, open tickets, trends, heat maps and response times are displayed here. Heat maps reveal department and hourly activity and average need by language.
For the Routing process, one ticket is generated for each department that has a need greater than its threshold value and the situations that lack a definite specific need are directed to the Disaster Management team. Department officials can mark and resolve these tickets. 
The only exception to this process of human validation is critical and high situations which, if not reviewed in 1 minute and 5 minutes respectively, are automatically routed and marked unverified, unless the situation is deemed likely to be a fake.
The prioritization of situations is done by applying the priority ranking that is based on the severity, actionability, corroboration, credibility and recency, and categorized as Critical, High, Medium or Low. It also produces the daily situation report.
5.2 Data Flow
Fig. 5.2 describes the high-level data flow. The social media posts are ingested into the system through ingestion endpoint where validation is done and the posts added to the queue. The background worker will consume and clean the posts by filtering out invalid posts and any duplicates indicating reasons for them. In terms of determining the language used in the post, the disaster classifier will analyze the post to establish whether the post contains information concerning any disaster. Non-disaster posts are filtered out of the process. The ones that qualify are subjected to sentiment analysis and departmental requirements predictions. These analyses are all compiled to form one analyzed post. All of the posts are converted to embedding and mapped to an event cluster. In situations whereby there are three or more posts within the event cluster, credibility, misinformation, and urgency levels are established before summarizing the information into a pending situation.

5.4 Low Level Design
This section describes the internal design of the backend. Design patterns shared by several modules are described once, followed by the logic specific to each stage of the pipeline.
5.4.1 Common Design Patterns
•	Request handling: every module follows the same structure. A router validates the request, a module function applies the logic, and a shared database helper (app/db.py) performs all storage operations.
•	Data contracts: data passed between modules is defined as Pydantic schemas, so invalid data is rejected at the boundary.
•	Model wrapper: the language, disaster, sentiment and need classifiers share one design. Each model is loaded once on first use, truncates input to a fixed length (128 tokens, or 96 for language identification), runs in inference mode and returns a plain dictionary. An offline stand-in with the same interface is used when the transformer libraries are unavailable.
•	Weighted scoring: every score lies in [0, 1] and is a weighted sum of named terms. A missing term is skipped and the remaining weights are renormalised. All weights and thresholds are defined in scoring/config.py.

7.3 APPLICATION START-UP AND CONFIGURATION

When the backend starts, it performs four steps before accepting traffic:

  1. It selects the model backend (trained models or offline stand-ins).
  2. It creates the MongoDB indexes. This operation is idempotent, so existing indexes are left unchanged.
  3. It restores the active clusters from MongoDB, so that posts about a known event continue to join the same cluster after a restart.
  4. It starts the background worker that processes the queue.

If MongoDB cannot be reached during steps 2 and 3, a warning is logged and the API still starts. Deployment settings are read from environment variables, as listed in Table 7.2. Scoring weights and thresholds are kept separately in scoring/config.py.

7.4 INGESTION AND THE STREAMING QUEUE

Posts enter the system through POST /ingestion/ingest. Pydantic validates each request: the text must contain between 1 and 1,000 characters, and any additional source fields are kept as attributes. The post is then assigned a unique identifier and an ingestion timestamp, and is placed on the queue.

The queue is an in-memory double-ended queue. When it is full, the oldest post is removed so that the newest reports are always retained, and the API response reports that this has happened. A single background task, started with the application, removes posts from the queue one at a time. Algorithm 7.1 shows both operations.

Algorithm 7.1: Bounded Queue and Background Worker

  procedure ENQUEUE(item)
      droppedOldest <- false
      if size(Q) >= QUEUE_MAX_SIZE then
          remove the oldest item from Q
          droppedOldest <- true
      append item to Q
      return droppedOldest

  procedure WORKER()
      lastCheck <- 0
      loop forever
          if now() - lastCheck >= ESCALATION_CHECK_SECONDS then
              lastCheck <- now()
              ESCALATE-OVERDUE()                  // Algorithm 7.11
              STORE-PREVIOUS-DAY-REPORT()
          item <- remove the oldest item from Q
          if item is empty then
              sleep(PIPELINE_POLL_INTERVAL)
          else
              PROCESS-POST(item)                  // Algorithm 7.2

The periodic checks run even when no posts are arriving, so an urgent situation is still escalated during a quiet period.


7.5 PIPELINE ORCHESTRATION

The PipelineOrchestrator class connects all processing stages. Model inference is CPU-intensive, so the analysis stages run in a separate thread through asyncio.to_thread, which keeps the API responsive while a post is being processed. A lock allows only one post at a time to change the in-memory clusters. Database writes then take place asynchronously. Algorithm 7.2 shows the complete flow for one post.

Algorithm 7.2: Processing One Post

  Input : ingested event e
  Output: final stage reached by the post

  procedure PROCESS-POST(e)
      text <- CLEAN-TEXT(e.text)                      // Algorithm 7.3
      if text contains no letter or digit then
          log as dropped (invalid); return "invalid"
      acquire lock
      if REGISTER-REPOST(text) > 1 then
          log as dropped (repost); return "repost"
      lang <- DETECT-LANGUAGE(text)                   // Algorithm 7.4
      pDisaster <- DISASTER-MODEL(text)
      if pDisaster < threshold then
          log as dropped (not_disaster); return "non_disaster"
      sentiment <- SENTIMENT-MODEL(text)
      needs <- NEED-MODEL(text)                       // Algorithm 7.5
      record <- merge(e, lang, pDisaster, sentiment, needs)
      C <- ASSIGN-TO-CLUSTER(record)                  // Algorithm 7.6
      clock <- max(clock, record.created_at)
      if size(C) >= 3 then
          situation <- BUILD-SITUATION(C, clock)      // Algorithms 7.7 to 7.9
      release lock
      store record and cluster state in MongoDB
      if situation exists then
          insert or update the situation for cluster C
          return "situation"
      return "holding"

Three implementation decisions make the pipeline reliable:

  a) Failure isolation: PROCESS-POST catches every exception, records it in the pipeline statistics and returns the stage "error". A malformed post therefore cannot stop the stream.
  b) Safe reprocessing: posts are written using their identifier as the key, and each cluster has exactly one situation. Replaying the same data updates existing documents instead of creating duplicates.
  c) Protected review state: a rescore can change only analytical fields such as scores, summary and evidence. The review status and decision are never overwritten. The last 20 scores are kept in a score history, which the dashboard uses to display the trend in priority.

Recency is measured against a simulation clock, which holds the newest post time seen so far. Replayed historical data is therefore scored in the same way as live data.


7.6 PREPROCESSING AND LANGUAGE DETECTION

Algorithm 7.3 shows text cleaning and repost detection. Regular-expression word classes are deliberately avoided when removing symbols, because they do not match Indic vowel signs and viramas and would break words written in Indian scripts. Each character is instead retained or removed according to its Unicode category.

Algorithm 7.3: Text Cleaning and Repost Detection

  procedure CLEAN-TEXT(text)
      text <- repair broken Unicode encoding (ftfy)
      text <- replace each emoji with its text description
      remove URLs and @mentions
      for each character ch in text do
          if ch is not a letter, digit, combining mark, space or
                  permitted punctuation mark then
              replace ch with a space
      collapse repeated whitespace
      return text

  procedure REGISTER-REPOST(text)
      normalised <- lowercase text without a leading "RT" marker
      fingerprint <- SHA-256(normalised)
      count[fingerprint] <- count[fingerprint] + 1
      return count[fingerprint]

Language detection follows the two-stage approach shown in Algorithm 7.4. Text in a native script is identified directly from its Unicode range without running a model. The trained classifier is used only for Latin-script text, where English and romanised Indian languages cannot be told apart by script.

Algorithm 7.4: Two-Stage Language Detection

  procedure DETECT-LANGUAGE(text)
      for each lang in {hi, kn, ta, te} do
          count[lang] <- number of characters of text in the script of lang
      best <- language with the highest count
      if count[best] >= 3 then
          lang <- best
          romanised <- false
      else
          lang <- LANGUAGE-MODEL(text)
          romanised <- (lang != en)
      latin <- number of Latin letters in text
      codeMixed <- (count[best] >= 3) and (latin >= 5)
      return lang, romanised, codeMixed


7.7 MODEL INFERENCE

Five pretrained models are used during inference, as listed in Table 7.3. The training of these models is described in Section 7.17.

Table 7.3: Models Used for Inference

  Task               | Model repository                             | Output                                 | Max tokens
  -------------------|----------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------|-----------
  Language detection | Favaz-07/language-detection-xlmr             | One of five languages                  | 96
  Disaster filter    | Favaz-07/disaster-classifier-xlmr            | Probability that a post is disaster-related | 128
  Sentiment          | akshataudaganve/xlmr-sentiment-disaster      | Negative and positive probabilities    | 128
  Need detection     | Favaz-07/need-detection-xlmr                 | Independent probability for four needs | 128
  Sentence embedding | l3cube-pune/indic-sentence-similarity-sbert  | 768-dimensional normalised vector      | Model default

All four classifiers use the same wrapper, illustrated by the need-detection model in Algorithm 7.5. A model is loaded once, on first use, and remains in memory for later posts. Need detection is a multi-label task, so a sigmoid function is applied to each output, allowing a post to indicate several needs at once. The disaster and sentiment classifiers use a softmax function, and the language classifier selects the class with the highest score.

Algorithm 7.5: Model Inference (Need Detection)

  procedure NEED-MODEL(text)
      if model is not loaded then
          source <- local model folder if present, else Hugging Face Hub
          load tokenizer and model from source
          set model to evaluation mode
      tokens <- tokenizer(text, max_length = 128, truncate, pad)
      without gradient tracking do
          logits <- model(tokens)
      p <- sigmoid(logits)
      needs <- {healthcare: p1, municipal: p2, tourism: p3, law: p4}
      return needs, max(needs)

The script tools/download_models.py stores each model in backend/models and verifies every weight file against its SHA-256 checksum, after which the backend can run without network access. If the deep-learning libraries are not installed, an offline backend replaces the prediction functions with keyword and hashing heuristics. This allows the rest of the pipeline to be developed and demonstrated on a machine without the models. Its outputs are not predictions from the trained models.


7.8 INCREMENTAL CLUSTERING

The ClusterManager class holds the live clusters in memory. Algorithm 7.6 applies the four joining conditions from Section 5.3.4 and selects the most similar eligible cluster.

Algorithm 7.6: Incremental Cluster Assignment

  Input : analysed record r, list of live clusters L
  Output: the cluster containing r

  procedure ASSIGN-TO-CLUSTER(r)
      v <- normalise(IndicSBERT(r.text))
      best <- none;  bestSim <- -1
      for each cluster C in L do
          sim <- dot(v, C.centroid)
          if sim < 0.32 then continue
          if |r.time - C.lastUpdated| > 48 hours then continue
          if no department has need > 0.3 in both r and C then continue
          if r and C both name places and share none then continue
          if sim > bestSim then
              best <- C;  bestSim <- sim
      if best is none then
          create a new cluster containing r and add it to L
          return the new cluster
      add r to best
      best.centroid <- mean of all member embeddings
      for each department d do
          best.relevance[d] <- max(best.relevance[d], r.needs[d])
      return best

Place names are compared using a single spelling for each place, so that "Mysuru" and "Mysore" are treated as the same location. Clusters that have become similar over time can be merged through POST /clustering/recluster, which applies HDBSCAN [23] to the cluster centroids. This operation is run on demand and is not part of the automatic worker loop.

Cluster state is saved each time a post joins a cluster. Each centroid is stored in a compact 32-bit binary format, and member embeddings are kept in the tweets collection, so that a large cluster cannot exceed MongoDB's 16 MB document limit. After every 100 posts, clusters older than the 48-hour window are marked inactive and their member embeddings are deleted, which limits the storage used by vectors. At start-up, clusters created with a different embedding model are ignored, so vectors from different models are never compared.


7.9 CLUSTER SCORING

Scoring is implemented in the scoring package using only explicit rules and formulas. Multilingual keyword lists cover urgency, severity, location, place names, infrastructure, time, forwarding language and hearsay in English, Hindi, Kannada, Tamil and Telugu. Except for place names, each list also contains romanised forms of the Indian-language terms.

Before scoring, a cluster's posts are grouped into independent reports, as shown in Algorithm 7.7. A union-find structure is used so that copying is treated as transitive: if post A matches post B and post B matches post C, all three belong to one group.

Algorithm 7.7: Grouping Copies into Independent Reports

  procedure GROUP-REPORTS(members)
      reports <- members, keeping one post for each origin_id
      initialise a union-find structure over reports
      for each pair of reports (a, b) do
          if normalised text of a = normalised text of b
                  or cosine(a.embedding, b.embedding) >= 0.92 then
              UNION(a, b)
      return reports, the groups of the union-find structure

Algorithm 7.8 calculates the final scores for a cluster. Every score uses the weighted-sum helper described in Section 5.3.1, so a missing input is skipped and the remaining weights are renormalised.

Algorithm 7.8: Cluster Scoring

  Input : cluster members, simulation clock now
  Output: priority, band, fake score, likely-fake flag

  procedure SCORE-CLUSTER(members, now)
      reports, groups <- GROUP-REPORTS(members)
      for each post p in members do
          compute severity, actionability, text quality, specificity,
              content risk and model confidence of p
          if |groups| <= 1 then consistency(p) <- 0.5
          else consistency(p) <- scaled similarity of p to the centroid
          credibility(p) <- WEIGHTED-SUM(terms of p)
      independent <- first post of each group
      S <- mean of the three highest severity values in members
      A <- mean of the three highest actionability values in members
      Cr <- mean credibility over independent
      R <- mean content risk over reports
      Co <- WEIGHTED-SUM(report volume, language diversity)
      Am <- WEIGHTED-SUM(duplicate ratio, copy burst, forward ratio)
      Re <- 0.5 ^ (hours since the latest post / 6)
      priority <- WEIGHTED-SUM(S, A, Co, Cr, Re)
      fake <- WEIGHTED-SUM(1 - Cr, R, Am)
      band <- first of Critical, High, Medium, Low whose minimum is met
      return priority, band, fake, (fake >= 0.60)


7.10 SUMMARY, EVIDENCE AND PRIVACY

The summary is assembled in two steps. Language-neutral facts such as the event type, location, impact, number of independent reports and main needs are first extracted from the cluster. The same facts are then written into a sentence template for each of the five languages. A fact that is not available is omitted from the sentence.

Evidence posts are chosen by Algorithm 7.9, which favours high-scoring posts while keeping the selection varied across languages. Each selected post is stored with a short reason, such as "closest to event" or "location + number", which is displayed to the reviewer. Evidence posts are shown in their original language.

Algorithm 7.9: Evidence Selection

  procedure SELECT-EVIDENCE(members, groups)
      candidates <- earliest post of each group
      remove posts with forwarding language from candidates,
          unless this would remove every post
      rank candidates by descending EvidenceScore
      k <- min(5, size(candidates))
      selected <- empty list
      while size(selected) < k do
          remaining <- ranked candidates not yet selected
          choice <- first post in remaining whose language is not yet selected
          if no such post exists then
              choice <- first post whose language appears fewer than 2 times
          if no such post exists then
              choice <- first post in remaining
          add choice to selected with the reason for its selection
      return selected

Privacy controls are applied before data is written to the database:

  - Author fields such as usernames and user identifiers are removed as soon as analysis begins. They are replaced by a salted SHA-256 reference, which can distinguish authors but cannot be reversed to reveal them.
  - Digit sequences containing at least seven digits are replaced by [PHONE_REDACTED]. Shorter numbers, such as casualty counts, are retained.
  - Metadata fields containing an address or location are replaced by [ADDRESS_REDACTED].


7.11 REVIEW, ROUTING AND ESCALATION

A reviewer's decision is processed by Algorithm 7.10. Every decision is stored as a verdict together with a frozen copy of the scores at that moment, so later rescoring does not alter the record used for evaluation. Ticket creation is idempotent: repeating an approval does not create a second ticket, and a unique database index on the situation and department provides a further safeguard.

Algorithm 7.10: Reviewer Decision and Ticket Creation

  procedure DECIDE(s, decision, note)
      if decision = approve then
          OPEN-TICKETS(s, verified = true, flag = none)
      else if decision = fake then
          OPEN-TICKETS(s, verified = false, flag = "fake")
      else                                        // reject
          cancel all open tickets of s
      update the status of s and record the decision time
      store a verdict with a snapshot of the scores of s

  procedure OPEN-TICKETS(s, verified, flag)
      departments <- departments of all needs above their thresholds
      if departments is empty then
          departments <- {department assigned to s}
      for each department d in departments do
          t <- ticket for (s, d)
          if t does not exist then
              create a ticket for d with status routed
          else
              if t is cancelled then reopen t with status routed
              update the verified and flag fields of t

Algorithm 7.11 runs every 10 seconds. Each High or Critical situation receives a review deadline when it is created. If its band later rises, the earlier deadline is retained, and if the band falls to Medium or Low, the deadline is removed. Before opening tickets, the algorithm changes the status only if the situation is still pending. If a reviewer has decided in the meantime, the update has no effect and no ticket is opened.

Algorithm 7.11: Automatic Escalation

  procedure ESCALATE-OVERDUE()
      overdue <- situations with status pending, a deadline that has
                 passed, and likely_fake not set
      for each situation s in overdue do
          claimed <- update s to auto_routed only if s is still pending
          if claimed then
              OPEN-TICKETS(s, verified = false, flag = none)

Department officers then move each ticket through its lifecycle. The API accepts only the transitions shown in Figure 5.8 and returns HTTP 409 for any other request, such as acknowledging a ticket that has already been resolved.

7.17 DATA PREPARATION AND MODEL TRAINING

The four classifiers in Table 7.3 were fine-tuned from xlm-roberta-base [16] in Kaggle GPU notebooks, separately from the running system. IndicSBERT [18] was used as published and was not trained. Two datasets were prepared. The Disaster Tweets set contains 11,095 cleaned English tweets from Kaggle and 3,000 IndicTrans2 [19] translations in each of Hindi, Kannada, Tamil and Telugu, giving 23,095 rows. The multilingual tweet set contains 177,707 training rows and 18,540 test rows, drawn from Sentiment140 [21] for English, NLLB-200 [20] translations of it for Hindi and Telugu, and DravidianCodeMix [22] for Kannada and Tamil. Table 7.7 gives the final split sizes, with training counts shown after balancing.

Table 7.7: Training Data

  Model              | Dataset                | Train   | Validation | Test
  -------------------|------------------------|---------|------------|-------
  Disaster filter    | Disaster Tweets set    | 40,616  | 1,809      | 1,830
  Need detection     | Disaster Tweets set    | 38,180  | 2,329      | 2,342
  Language detection | Multilingual tweet set | 95,000  | 5,000      | 18,540
  Sentiment          | Multilingual tweet set | 159,936 | 17,771     | 18,540

Algorithm 7.12 shows the cleaning procedure as implemented for the SentiMix code-mixed corpus, which was used in the earlier Phase 2 experiments. The two final datasets followed the same main steps. In addition, disaster tweets were converted to lower case and special characters were removed, while the sentiment data was Unicode-normalised, repeated characters were reduced, and missing, very short and duplicate rows were dropped.

Algorithm 7.12: Social Media Text Cleaning

  procedure CLEAN-TEXT(t)
      t <- decode HTML entities and remove zero-width characters
      remove a leading "RT"
      remove URLs, broken t.co fragments and @mentions
      replace #word with word
      shorten runs of !, ? or . to two characters
      collapse repeated whitespace and remove trailing dots
      replace each emoji with its English name    // training split only
      return t                                    // empty rows are discarded

A translated tweet carries the same content as its English source, so a random split could place the two versions in different sets and inflate the test scores. Algorithm 7.13 prevents this by splitting on the identity of the original tweet. Department labels were derived from keywords in the English tweets, and each translation inherits the labels of its source.

Algorithm 7.13: Leakage-Safe Split and Language Balancing

  Input : English rows E, translated rows T
  Output: training, validation and test sets

  procedure PREPARE-DATA(E, T)
      assign a tweet_id to each row of E
      give each row of T the tweet_id and labels of its English source
      split the tweet_ids 80/10/10, stratified by label
      place every row in the split that holds its tweet_id
      assert that no tweet_id occurs in two splits
      add two extra copies of each non-English training row
      return train, validation, test    // validation and test stay unbalanced

For need detection, 32 hand-written English examples were each added five times, and rows labelled tourism, the rarest need, were tripled. For language detection, 40 duplicate rows and 59 rows that also appeared in the test file were removed from the training data. Each language was then resampled to 20,000 rows, and 5% of the result was held out for validation. Sentiment used a 90/10 split of the training file, stratified by language and label, without this resampling or row removal. Algorithm 7.14 shows the training procedure, and Table 7.8 lists the settings for each model.

Algorithm 7.14: Model Training and Evaluation

  procedure TRAIN-MODEL(train, validation, test, config)
      model <- xlm-roberta-base with a new output layer for the task
      tokens <- tokenizer(text, truncate and pad to config.maxTokens)
      for epoch <- 1 to config.epochs do
          for each batch in train do
              loss <- config.loss(model(batch), batch.labels)
              update the weights with AdamW
          score <- evaluate the model on validation
          if score is the best so far then save a checkpoint
      restore the best checkpoint
      if the task is need detection then
          for each label do
              threshold <- value from 0.20 to 0.80 (step 0.05) with the best validation F1
      report the metrics on test
      save the model and tokenizer

Table 7.8: Training Configuration and Test Results

  Model     | Max tokens | Epochs                  | Batch per GPU | Loss                                   | Test result
  ----------|------------|-------------------------|---------------|----------------------------------------|--------------------------------
  Language  | 96         | 2                       | 32            | Cross-entropy                          | Accuracy 99.24%, macro F1 0.983
  Disaster  | 128        | 3                       | 16            | Class-weighted cross-entropy           | Accuracy 89.02%, F1 0.710
  Sentiment | 128        | Up to 6, early stopping | 16            | Cross-entropy                          | Accuracy 82.75%, F1 0.852
  Need      | 128        | 3                       | 32            | Binary cross-entropy, pos_weight <= 10 | Exact match 92.66%, macro F1 0.67

All models used a learning rate of 2e-5, with warm-up over the first 10% of training steps. For sentiment, MuRIL [17] was trained in the same way, and XLM-R was selected for its higher test F1 (0.852 against 0.849). The need thresholds chosen on the validation set (healthcare 0.65, municipal 0.80, tourism 0.75, law 0.65) are the values used by the backend. Per-language disaster thresholds were also explored, ranging from 0.07 for English to above 0.99 for the other languages. These values were not adopted, and the backend applies a single threshold of 0.5.


SUMMARY
This chapter described the implementation of the system. A FastAPI backend processes each post through an asynchronous, fault-tolerant pipeline covering cleaning, language detection, model inference, incremental clustering, scoring, summarisation and evidence selection. Human review, ticket routing and automatic escalation were implemented with safeguards against duplicate tickets and conflicting decisions, and a React frontend presents the results to reviewers and departments. The classifiers were trained offline on multilingual data using leakage-safe splits. An automated suite of 165 test cases verifies the system's logic. The performance of the models and the complete system is evaluated in the next chapter.

8.1 EVALUATION SETUP AND METRICS

Each classifier was evaluated once on a held-out test set that was not used for training, balancing or threshold selection. Test sets kept their natural class and language distribution, and every result was also reported per language so that strong English performance could not hide weak performance elsewhere. The scoring formulas were evaluated on synthetic situations, because no human-labelled set of real situations was available. Table 8.1 defines the metrics used.

Table 8.1: Evaluation Metrics

  Metric                  | Meaning                                                       | Used for
  ------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------|---------------------------
  Accuracy                | Share of all predictions that are correct                     | All classifiers
  Precision               | Share of predicted positives that are truly positive          | All classifiers, fake score
  Recall                  | Share of true positives that are found                        | All classifiers, fake score
  F1 score                | Harmonic mean of precision and recall                         | Disaster, sentiment, fake score
  Macro-F1                | Unweighted mean of the F1 score of each class                 | Language, need detection
  Micro-F1                | F1 calculated from the pooled counts of all labels            | Need detection
  Exact-match accuracy    | Share of posts with every label predicted correctly           | Need detection
  Confusion matrix        | Counts of actual classes against predicted classes            | Language, sentiment, priority
  Exact band agreement    | Share of situations placed in the expected priority band      | Priority score
  Within-one-band         | Share of situations no more than one band from the expected   | Priority score
  Spearman correlation    | Agreement between priority order and expected urgency order   | Priority score
  False alarms            | Genuine situations wrongly flagged as likely fake             | Fake score
  Processing overhead     | Pipeline time per post, in milliseconds                       | System performance


8.2 MODEL RESULTS

8.3 CLUSTERING AND SCORING RESULTS

Clustering. The incremental clustering module passed all six functional scenarios: merging posts about one event, separating unrelated events, respecting the 48-hour window, merging English and Tamil reports of one flood, holding back small clusters, and running the HDBSCAN correction step. During calibration, same-event pairs had a mean cosine similarity of 0.51 and the different-event pair scored 0.08. Testing also exposed a department-overlap rule that wrongly blocked a flood report describing health effects, and the rule was corrected. These scenarios confirm correct behaviour, but clustering quality was not measured on a labelled dataset.

Priority score. The formula was evaluated on 52 synthetic situations whose expected bands were fixed in advance by a written rubric, with 30 used for tuning and 22 held out. Table 8.5 gives the held-out result. The final formula placed 54.5% of held-out situations in the exact band and all of them within one band. The Spearman correlation between priority and expected urgency across all 52 situations was 0.85, so the formula orders situations well even when a band boundary is missed. A search over weight combinations did not produce a more reliable formula: the best weights on the tuning set reached only 50% on the held-out set, and five-fold cross-validation averaged 54% for the final weights against 48% for tuned weights. Moving 0.05 of weight between any two terms kept agreement between 50% and 56%. Two of the four Critical situations were placed in the High band, so the Critical boundary remains conservative.

8.4 DISCUSSION

The closest published studies each address a single stage of crisis monitoring and use different datasets, languages and labels, so their results provide context rather than a direct ranking. For disaster detection, Algiriyage et al. [5] report an F1 score of about 0.92 with a Bi-LSTM on 192K English tweets, and PIDMUS [10] reaches 81.36% accuracy in English and 86.75% in Turkish. The disaster filter in this project achieved 86.11% to 90.56% accuracy across five languages, with F1 scores of 0.615 to 0.733. For need detection, Ray Chowdhury et al. [7] report F1 scores of 79.84 to 87.44 on mainly English and European tweets. This project's micro-F1 of 0.73 is lower, which is the cost of covering five languages with one model. IndicLID [9] reaches 98.55% accuracy on native script but only 80.40% on romanised text, confirming that romanised text is the harder case for language detection. In clustering and prioritisation, Ray et al. [13] use a similar threshold-based incremental method, CURD [14] classifies urgency with an F1 score of 0.88, and EventHunter [15] ranks events with a weighted score assessed only qualitatively. The priority score in this project was instead tested on held-out situations and for weight sensitivity.

The main gap in this work is integration. Indian-language studies such as Das et al. [8] stop at classification, and both the systems that cluster or rank posts [13], [14], [15] and multi-label crisis classifiers such as CrisisSense-LLM [12] work mainly in English. None of the surveyed works takes a post written in an Indian language through to a scored situation and a reviewed ticket for a department, and this is the gap the project fills.

The system also has clear limitations. Need detection is weak outside English, with macro-F1 between 0.376 and 0.553, so Indic posts may not reach the correct department. The Indic training data is machine-translated, and hand-written Hindi and Kannada flood messages were missed. This agrees with Rahul and Adebayo [11], who found that XLM-R accuracy fell from 99.7% to 42.5% under harder test conditions. Romanised and code-mixed posts were not evaluated separately. The fake-score threshold of 0.60 raised no false alarms but also caught none of the 21 synthetic rumours. Both scoring formulas were validated only on synthetic situations, and clustering only through six scenarios. Finally, the system replays a CSV file instead of reading live social media, so its performance under real traffic is untested.


SUMMARY
The language detector reached 99.24% accuracy, the disaster filter 89.02% with an F1 score of 0.710, the sentiment model 82.75% with an F1 score of 0.852, and need detection a micro-F1 of 0.73. The priority formula placed every held-out synthetic situation within one band of its expected level. Compared with published work, individual modules do not exceed the best single-task English systems, but no surveyed system combines Indian-language analysis, situation building, scoring and reviewed department routing. The main remaining weaknesses are non-English need detection, reliance on translated training data and the untuned fake-score threshold. These motivate the future work described in the next chapter.

9.1 CONCLUSION

This project set out to turn multilingual social media posts about a crisis into verified, prioritised work for the government departments that must respond. The resulting system accepts posts in English, Hindi, Kannada, Tamil and Telugu, including romanised text, and processes each post through cleaning, repost detection, language detection, disaster filtering, sentiment analysis and multi-label need detection. Related posts are grouped into situations using multilingual IndicSBERT embeddings, so that reports of one event in different languages are treated as one situation rather than separate alerts.

Each situation receives a priority score and a fake score calculated from explainable formulas. Every term in these formulas is visible to the reviewer, so the reason for a Critical band or a misinformation warning can be checked. A template-based summary is produced in all five languages, and the most representative posts are attached as evidence after personal details are masked. Human reviewers confirm each situation before routing, and a situation that concerns several departments opens a separate ticket for each one. Critical and High situations are routed automatically if no reviewer acts within the configured time, so urgent reports are not delayed by the review queue. Departments then acknowledge and resolve tickets, and the analytics dashboard and daily reports summarise response times, trends and workload.

The four classifiers achieved 99.24% accuracy for language detection, 89.02% accuracy for the disaster filter, 82.75% accuracy for sentiment and a micro-F1 of 0.73 for need detection. The priority formula placed every held-out synthetic situation within one band of its expected level, with a rank correlation of 0.85. All 165 automated tests passed. These results show that a single pipeline can connect Indian-language posts to departmental action, which no surveyed work attempted. The results also show where the system is weakest: need detection outside English, models trained on machine-translated text, and a fake-score threshold that has not yet been tuned on real data.

Table 9.1 summarises the outcome of each project objective.

Table 9.1: Project Objectives and Outcomes

  Objective                                         | Outcome
  --------------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------
  Multilingual analysis in five languages           | Achieved, including romanised text and code-mixing flags
  Sentiment and need detection                      | Achieved; need detection is weaker outside English
  Fake-news and priority scoring                    | Achieved with explainable formulas; fake threshold untuned
  Situation summaries and evidence                  | Achieved in all five languages, with privacy masking

  Dashboard monitoring and reports                  | Achieved: live board, trends, heatmaps and daily reports
  Real-time processing of live social media         | Partly achieved; posts are streamed from a CSV simulator


9.2 FUTURE WORK

The following extensions would address the limitations identified in Chapter 8 and move the system closer to deployment.

1. Learning from reviewer decisions. Every verdict is stored with a snapshot of the scores at the time of review. Once enough real decisions are collected, they can be used to re-tune the priority weights, the band cut-offs and the fake-score threshold. The synthetic evaluation suggests that a fake threshold near 0.40 would catch about half of the rumours without false alarms, but this should be confirmed on real data.

2. Native and code-mixed training data. The disaster and need models should be retrained on posts written natively in Indian languages, including romanised and code-mixed text, instead of machine translations. Need detection outside English should be the first priority, and romanised posts should be evaluated separately.

3. Per-language calibration. The system already supports per-language calibration of model confidence and per-language disaster thresholds, but neither has been fitted. Fitting them on a validation set would make scores comparable across languages.

4. Translation of evidence. A hook exists for translating non-English evidence into English with IndicTrans2, but it is not yet connected. Completing it would let reviewers who do not read every supported language verify the evidence directly.

5. Live data sources. The CSV simulator should be replaced by adapters for social media APIs, and the in-memory queue by a persistent message broker so that queued posts survive a restart.

6. Department access. Each department should have its own login and queue, so that officers see only their tickets and every action is linked to an accountable user.

7. Stronger evaluation. Clustering should be measured with ARI and AMI on a labelled event dataset, the HDBSCAN correction step should run on a schedule, and the models should be tested on adversarial and real crisis posts in addition to held-out benchmark data.

8. Wider coverage. Location mapping on the heatmap and support for further Indian languages would extend the system to more regions and users.

In summary, the project delivers a working, tested and explainable prototype that links multilingual public reports to departmental response. Its main value lies in this integration, and the future work above is aimed at making each stage reliable enough for use in a real crisis.

APPENDIX A
DEFINITIONS, ACRONYMS AND ABBREVIATIONS

ACRONYMS AND ABBREVIATIONS

API – Application Programming Interface
A set of rules and endpoints that allows different software components to communicate. The backend of this system exposes its functions through a REST API built with FastAPI.

NLP – Natural Language Processing
A field of artificial intelligence concerned with enabling computers to understand and process human language.

XLM-R – Cross-lingual Language Model – RoBERTa
A transformer model pretrained on text in 100 languages. It is the base model for the language, disaster, sentiment and need classifiers in this system.

mBERT – Multilingual BERT
A version of BERT pretrained on text in many languages, used in several of the surveyed studies.

MuRIL – Multilingual Representations for Indian Languages
A BERT-based model pretrained on Indian languages. It was trained as an alternative sentiment model and compared with XLM-R.

IndicSBERT
A sentence-embedding model for Indian languages that places sentences with similar meanings close together, even across languages. It is used to group related posts into clusters.

IndicTrans2
A machine translation model for the 22 scheduled languages of India. It was used to translate English training data into Hindi, Kannada, Tamil and Telugu.

HDBSCAN – Hierarchical Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise
A clustering algorithm that finds groups of different densities. It is used to merge clusters that have become similar over time.

LSTM – Long Short-Term Memory
A recurrent neural network that captures long-range dependencies in sequences, used by several of the surveyed studies.

TF-IDF – Term Frequency – Inverse Document Frequency
A statistical measure of how important a word is to a document within a collection of documents.

F1 Score
The harmonic mean of precision and recall. Macro-F1 averages the F1 score of each class equally, and micro-F1 calculates it from the pooled counts of all classes.

PII – Personally Identifiable Information
Information that can identify a person, such as a phone number or an email address. It is masked before evidence is stored.


TERMS

Code-Mixing
The use of two or more languages within the same sentence or conversation, for example Hindi and English in one post.

Romanised Text
Text in an Indian language written in the Latin alphabet instead of its native script, for example Hindi written as "madad chahiye".

Sentiment
The emotional tone of a text. It is often divided into positive, negative and neutral classes; this system classifies posts as negative or positive and uses the negative probability in scoring.

Need Detection
The identification of help requests or actionable needs in a post. In this system each post can indicate needs for several departments at once.

Situation
A group of three or more related posts that describe the same real-world event. Situations, not individual posts, are scored, reviewed and routed.

Priority Score
A value between 0 and 1 calculated from severity, actionability, corroboration, credibility and recency. It places each situation in the Critical, High, Medium or Low band.

Fake Score
A value between 0 and 1 calculated from low credibility, content risk and amplification risk. It estimates how likely a situation is to be misinformation.

Auto-Escalation
The automatic routing of a Critical or High situation to its departments when no reviewer acts within the configured time.

Ticket
A work item opened for a department when a situation is routed. A department acknowledges and then resolves each ticket.
